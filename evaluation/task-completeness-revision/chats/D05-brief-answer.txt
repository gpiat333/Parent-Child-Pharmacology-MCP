## Evidence brief: sertraline during lactation

**Identity:** The lookup matched the ingredient **sertraline** exactly (RxNorm 36437). The evidence concerns sertraline as a single ingredient; combination-product safety and interactions were not assessed. [S16](https://rxnav.nlm.nih.gov/REST/rxcui/36437/properties.json)

### Effect on the lactating person

- LactMed reports a small observation in which six patients reported decreased milk supply after starting sertraline; doses were not reported. This is an observation, not proof that sertraline caused the change. [S19](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)
- A small mixed-SSRI study reported delayed onset of milk secretory activation among SRI-treated mothers, but no statistically significant difference in feeding difficulties after day 4. Sertraline-specific results were limited because only one participant in that study was taking sertraline. [S19](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)
- The selected DailyMed product is **SERTRALINE HYDROCHLORIDE TABLET [REMEDYREPACK INC.]**. Its lactation section states that published data show low levels of sertraline and metabolites in human milk and that it found no data on effects on milk production. [S7](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=687ebdc4-6946-454e-8d46-3207488ccf32)
- The retrieved adult label extract included an adverse-reactions section, but warnings, warnings and precautions, boxed warning, and drug-interactions sections were missing in the returned extraction. Missing sections are a limitation of this lookup, not evidence that those warnings do not exist elsewhere in the full labeling. [S7](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=687ebdc4-6946-454e-8d46-3207488ccf32)

### Measured infant exposure

- LactMed reports that sertraline is often not detected in infant serum, while norsertraline may be detectable at low levels. [S19](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)
- In the returned PubMed abstract for a study of **12 lactating women and 11 mother–infant pairs**—a human milk/infant-serum pharmacokinetic study—sertraline was detected in three nursing infants and norsertraline in six. The abstract also states that detected infant serum concentrations were below the detection limit of most commercial laboratories. [S15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9286185/)
- The DailyMed label describes a pooled analysis of **53 mother–infant pairs** in which exclusively human-milk-fed infants had lower serum concentrations than their mothers; the label reports an average infant-to-mother serum-level ratio of 2%, with a reported range of 0%–15%. [S7](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=687ebdc4-6946-454e-8d46-3207488ccf32)

These are research measurements of exposure, not dosing instructions or individualized exposure estimates.

### Observed infant outcomes

- In small observational samples summarized by LactMed, some infants had no observed acute adverse reactions, and one group had reported normal six-month weight gain. These findings are limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous study designs, and incomplete population details. [S19](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)
- LactMed describes a **preterm infant born at 33 weeks’ gestation** whose mother used sertraline during pregnancy and postpartum. The infant developed hyperthermia, abnormal muscle-tone regulation, and high-pitched crying. The source reports elevated infant serum concentrations and describes the reaction as probably caused by sertraline in the context of prematurity and reduced metabolism. This is a single case report and should not be generalized to all breastfed infants. [S19](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)
- A separate report described sedation in an infant whose mother was taking sertraline along with other medicines, so attribution to sertraline alone is not established. [S19](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)
- The retrieved LactMed exposure and infant-outcome sections were truncated. Some chronology and outcomes are therefore missing from this extract; no conclusion should be drawn from the omitted material.

### Literature and evidence limitations

- The PubMed search returned 129 records, but only a bounded relevance subset and abstracts were retrieved. It was not a systematic review, and several records did not establish a lactation-specific population. [S8](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/esearch.fcgi?db=pubmed&term=%28%28%22sertraline%22%5BMeSH+Terms%5D+OR+%22sertraline%22%5BSubstance+Name%5D+OR+%22sertraline%22%5BTitle%2FAbstract%5D%29%29+AND+%28%22Lactation%22%5BMeSH+Terms%5D+OR+%22Breast+Feeding%22%5BMeSH+Terms%5D+OR+%22Milk%2C+Human%22%5BMeSH+Terms%5D+OR+lactat%2A%5BTitle%2FAbstract%5D+OR+breastfeed%2A%5BTitle%2FAbstract%5D+OR+breastfed%5BTitle%2FAbstract%5D%29&retmax=5&sort=relevance&retmode=json)
- The returned evidence-base metadata did not establish a study count; missing study counts are unknown, not zero. Reference counts are not study counts. [S19](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)
- Source populations vary. The PubMed milk/infant-serum study has mixed returned population labels—pregnancy, lactation, and neonate—while some other records have population fields missing in the returned metadata. Findings should not be generalized beyond the reported populations. [S15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9286185/)
- The DailyMed record represents one selected product label, not every sertraline formulation, and its pregnancy section was truncated. The label effective date was November 5, 2026; this is a source-document date, not a guarantee that it is the latest approved labeling. [S7](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=687ebdc4-6946-454e-8d46-3207488ccf32)
- FAERS results were not lactation- or infant-specific. They are reporting volume, not incidence or causation, and cannot establish risk for all infants or all exposed people.

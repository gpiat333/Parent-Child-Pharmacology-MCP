### Measured exposure

LactMed reports sertraline and its metabolite norsertraline in breast milk in several small pharmacokinetic studies:

- **15 mothers:** mean milk sertraline concentration **45 mcg/L**; range **7–207 mcg/L**.
- **26 breastfeeding women**, approximately 5–36 weeks postpartum, studied with extensive 24-hour sampling: mean milk concentrations were **129 mcg/L** for sertraline (range **11–938 mcg/L**) and **258 mcg/L** for norsertraline (range **20–1,498 mcg/L**).
- **4 mothers at 2 months postpartum:** mean milk concentrations were **26.4 mcg/L** for sertraline and **29 mcg/L** for norsertraline.
- **One case involving a preterm infant:** breast-milk concentrations averaged **201 mcg/L** for sertraline and **358 mcg/L** for norsertraline; sampling times were unspecified.
- Other reports found sertraline concentrations of **26 and 46 mcg/L** in foremilk and hindmilk, respectively, at a stated sampling time; **11.3 mcg/L** median in six mothers, with a range of **3.6–35.7 mcg/L**; and **87 mcg/L** average in 15 mothers, with a range of **12–288 mcg/L**. Some sampling times were random or incompletely reported.

Infant specimen measurements were also reported. In a group of **30 breastfed infants aged 6–13 weeks**, 22 had undetectable sertraline in infant serum; eight had detectable levels averaging **7.9 mcg/L**. In a separate report of three infants, infant serum sertraline was undetectable in two; norsertraline was detectable below the reported assay threshold in one. The source does not provide the complete pooled infant-serum results in the returned extract. [S11](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

These are measured exposure findings, not administered-dose recommendations and not clinical outcomes.

### Observed infant outcomes

The retrieved LactMed record describes:

- **26 infants:** no detectable acute adverse reactions in the cited study.
- **14 mother–infant pairs:** no adverse effects were reported, alongside infant serotonin measurements.
- **25 infants followed through six months:** reported typical weight gain and no abnormal effects reported by their mothers.
- **One 4-month-old infant:** benign sleep myoclonus and transient agitation were reported.
- **A retrospective study of 30 nursing mothers:** adverse effects were reported in **5 infants (13%)**, most commonly insomnia and restlessness; constant crying and poor feeding were less common. This was a small, retrospective, uncontrolled study, and the source does not establish that sertraline caused every reported symptom.
- **One preterm infant born at 33 weeks:** after maternal sertraline exposure during pregnancy and postpartum, the infant developed hyperthermia, muscle-tone regulation problems, and high-pitched crying. The report described infant serum drug concentrations and an intermediate-metabolism genotype; the authors attributed the event to serotonergic overstimulation associated with persistent exposure and reduced metabolism. This is a single case report, and the returned extract does not establish how broadly it applies.
- **One infant exposed to sertraline plus other medicines:** sedation was reported, so the contribution of sertraline cannot be separated from co-exposures.
- In a small study of infants of depressed mothers, blunted pain responses were reported after prenatal and/or breastfeeding SSRI exposure; only four infants were exposed to sertraline, and the comparison group did not include depressed mothers who were not taking medication. The role of maternal depression or related factors therefore remained unresolved.

### Lactation and maternal context

The source reports six mothers who described decreased milk supply after starting sertraline, but this was an observational report without a comparison group. A small study of serotonin-reuptake inhibitors—not sertraline alone—reported delayed onset of milk secretory activation, without a statistically significant difference in feeding difficulties after day four. Other observational studies involved SSRIs or antidepressants as groups rather than sertraline alone, and the source notes that underlying psychiatric illness may also affect breastfeeding outcomes. These findings concern lactation or breastfeeding behavior, not infant milk-drug concentrations. [S11](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

### Evidence limitations

- The returned record does not establish a total study count; its cited evidence includes small pharmacokinetic studies, observational studies, case reports, and a meta-analysis.
- Milk concentrations vary with postpartum timing, sampling time, foremilk/hindmilk status, and maternal serum concentration.

- Prenatal exposure, maternal illness, co-medications, prematurity, and possible metabolic differences can complicate interpretation.
- Measured milk or infant-serum concentrations do not by themselves establish safety or harm.

### Measured exposure

The retrieved LactMed record reports measured **breast-milk** concentrations, including:

- In a pooled analysis of 15 mothers: sertraline **45 mcg/L** (range **7–207 mcg/L**).
- In 26 women studied with extensive milk sampling: sertraline **129 mcg/L** (range **11–938 mcg/L**) and norsertraline **258 mcg/L** (range **20–1,498 mcg/L**).
- In four mothers sampled at two months postpartum: sertraline **26.4 mcg/L** and norsertraline **29 mcg/L**.
- In one mother, foremilk contained **26 mcg/L** and hindmilk **46 mcg/L**.
- In nine women providing early-postpartum trough samples: sertraline was **<5 mcg/L** in one woman and **11.1–24.5 mcg/L** in the other eight.
- In 15 mothers with repeated milk collection: mean sertraline concentration was **87 mcg/L** (range **12–288 mcg/L**).

These are research measurements of milk exposure, not administered-dose guidance. ### Observed infant outcomes

The LactMed extract describes:

- No detectable acute adverse reactions in **26 infants**, with an average age of about 16.6 weeks, in one study.
- No clinical adverse reactions in **six infants aged 5–34 weeks** in another study.
- No adverse effects reported in **seven four-week-old infants** in one report.
- In a follow-up of **25 infants**, mothers reported no abnormal effects and infant six-month weight gain was described as within national growth standards.
- One **4-month-old infant** had benign neonatal sleep myoclonus and spontaneously resolving agitation.
- One case involved a **preterm infant born at 33 weeks** whose mother used sertraline during pregnancy and postpartum. The infant developed hyperthermia, abnormal muscle-tone regulation, and high-pitched crying during the first days of life. On day 5, infant serum concentrations of sertraline and its metabolite were reported to be within the therapeutic range reported for adults. The report linked the episode to serotonergic overstimulation in the setting of reduced metabolism. The infant’s symptoms dissipated after breastfeeding was discontinued on day 9, and serum drug levels decreased. This is a single case report; it does not establish frequency or causation for all preterm infants.
- A separate report described sedation in an infant whose mother was taking sertraline together with zopiclone and occasional alprazolam; the contribution of sertraline alone is therefore not established from that report.
- A small study reported blunted pain responses among infants exposed to an SSRI during pregnancy and/or through breast milk. Four of 30 infants were exposed to sertraline. The comparison group differed in maternal depression status, and the source stated that effects related to maternal behavior or depression could not be ruled out.

These findings are not all from the same study, and the retrieved LactMed infant-outcome section is truncated. [S11](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

### Lactation and milk-production findings

The DailyMed lactation section states that published literature shows low levels of sertraline and metabolites in human milk and reports a pooled analysis of **53 mother–infant pairs** in which exclusively human-milk-fed infants had average sertraline serum levels equal to 2% of maternal serum levels; no adverse reactions were observed in that group. The same label states that data on effects on milk production were unavailable. [S1](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=687ebdc4-6946-454e-8d46-3207488ccf32)

LactMed reports:

- Six patients described decreased milk supply after starting sertraline; this was an uncontrolled observation and maternal treatment details were not fully reported.
- A small prospective study of serotonin-reuptake inhibitors included only one sertraline-exposed participant, so its lactation findings were not sertraline-specific.
- Larger observational studies involving SSRIs or antidepressants reported differences in breastfeeding outcomes, but they included multiple medicines and underlying psychiatric illness. These studies do not establish that sertraline itself caused the observed breastfeeding differences.

Milk-production or breastfeeding findings concern the lactating person or breastfeeding process; they are separate from measured drug concentrations in milk and from infant clinical outcomes. [S11](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

### Limitations and source context

- The retrieved LactMed record mentions case reports, observational studies, and a meta-analysis, but a formal study count is **not established from this extract**.
- Sample sizes are often small, and studies vary in infant age, prematurity, sampling timing, extent of breastfeeding, prenatal exposure, and co-exposures.
- The LactMed “Drug Levels” and “Effects in Breastfed Infants” sections are truncated; additional details may be missing.
- A lack of observed effects in small studies is not proof of safety or proof that the population is well studied.
- The DailyMed record is a selected product label, not a confirmed match to a particular product. Its retrieved adverse-reaction section concerns the medicine-taker label population and should not be treated as a list of breastfed-infant outcomes. Several expected label sections were missing from the returned extraction, including warnings, boxed warning, warnings and precautions, and drug interactions; this is a retrieval limitation, not evidence that those sections are absent.

### Maternal pharmacokinetics and fetal/infant exposure

The label reports that pregnancy-related physiological changes may affect lamotrigine concentrations and therapeutic effect, with reported decreases during pregnancy and restoration toward pre-pregnancy concentrations after delivery. This is source labeling, not an individualized treatment plan. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

A naturalistic study of **60 mothers** measured psychotropic medications in maternal serum and breast milk at multiple time points and followed exposed children during the first 12 months. The abstract reported a relatively high breast-milk concentration-to-dose ratio for lamotrigine, but it did not provide a verified lamotrigine-specific numeric concentration, fetal concentration, or cord-blood concentration in the retrieved text. The study population was mixed—pregnancy, lactation, neonates, and children—and the returned metadata did not establish a lamotrigine-specific subgroup size. ### Fetal and infant outcomes

**Major congenital malformations — human registry data:** The selected label reports first-trimester lamotrigine-monotherapy registry findings of:

- **2.2%** among **1,558** infants in the International Lamotrigine Pregnancy Registry;
- **2.0%** among **1,562** infants in the North American Antiepileptic Drug Pregnancy Registry;
- **2.9%** among **2,514** exposures in EURAP, an international registry.

The label states that overall frequencies were similar to general-population estimates, while noting that outcome definitions, ascertainment, and comparator groups limit interpretation. These are registry observations, not proof of causation or safety. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

**Isolated oral clefts:** The same label reports that the North American registry observed **3.2 per 1,000** oral clefts among **2,200** infants exposed early in pregnancy, described in that label as approximately threefold higher than unexposed healthy controls. The label also states that this signal was not observed in other large international registries. A European case-control analysis reported an adjusted odds ratio of **1.45** with a 95% confidence interval of **0.8–2.63**. Thus, the retrieved sources contain an inconsistent oral-cleft signal rather than a single settled estimate. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

The completed EUROCAT registry record describes a case-control study of first-trimester lamotrigine monotherapy exposure and isolated oral clefts; its registry summary states that it found no statistically significant increased risk compared with other defects. Its comparison population was malformed infants/fetuses rather than a general unexposed population, and the registry record is not a substitute for a full published report. [S4](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01055327)

**Other outcomes:** The label states that meta-analyses evaluated fetal death, stillbirth, preterm birth, small-for-gestational-age birth, and neurodevelopmental delay, but that differences in outcome definitions, ascertainment, and comparator groups limit conclusions. The retrieved label extract does not provide study counts for these analyses; counts are therefore **missing in the exported data**, not zero. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

A mixed psychotropic-medication study reported no significant differences in birth outcomes between exposed and nonexposed newborns and no significant differences in development during the first 12 months. Because the study included multiple medications and the retrieved abstract does not establish a lamotrigine-specific outcome subgroup, this result should not be generalized as a lamotrigine-only finding. [S22](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36103361/)

**Animal evidence:** The label separately reports developmental toxicity and altered fetal folate in animal studies. These are preclinical findings and should not be merged with the human registry findings. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

### Questions for the clinician

1. How should the risks of the underlying condition—epilepsy or bipolar disorder—be separated from medication-associated findings?
2. Which comparator best fits the evidence: healthy controls, disease-matched controls, or another antiseizure medication?
3. How applicable are the registry findings to the exact formulation, monotherapy/polytherapy context, co-medications, gestational timing, and pregnancy history?
4. How should the inconsistent oral-cleft findings across registries and case-control data be interpreted?
5. What human exposure measurements are available for the relevant pregnancy stage, and which are actually measured rather than modeled or inferred?
6. Which fetal structural, growth, perinatal, and later neurodevelopmental outcomes are most relevant to discuss given the evidence gaps?
7. Would pregnancy-registry participation be relevant as a research-information option, recognizing that registry status does not establish eligibility, safety, or benefit?

## Sertraline during lactation: balanced research summary

The evidence supports **low transfer into milk in many studied lactation samples**, but it is not a safety verdict. Infant outcomes and measured exposure are separate types of evidence.

### Measured milk and infant exposure

- A 1997 pharmacokinetic study of **12 lactating women and 11 mother–infant pairs** measured sertraline and desmethylsertraline in milk and infant serum. **Study type:** human pharmacokinetic observational study. **Source population:** pregnancy, lactation, and neonate in the returned metadata. Both compounds were found in milk; sertraline was detected in 3 infants’ serum and desmethylsertraline in 6. The study reported no observed adverse effects in those infants. Milk concentrations varied with timing and with foremilk versus hindmilk. [S19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9286185/)

- LactMed summarizes additional small observational studies, case reports, and a meta-analysis. Many reported low or undetectable infant serum concentrations, but the retrieved record does not establish a study count; its reference count is not a study count. Some sampling times, breastfeeding amounts, and other design details were incomplete. These limitations make the evidence less precise for uncommon or long-term outcomes. [S21](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

- A 2023 **naturalistic observational study** included **60 mothers** and followed exposed children during the first 12 months. **Source population:** pregnancy, lactation, neonate, and child; the returned metadata does not establish a lactation-only or sertraline-only subgroup size. The abstract reports pharmacokinetic measurements for several psychotropic medicines, including sertraline, but does not provide a separate sertraline-specific infant milk concentration. [S14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36103361/)

### Observed infant outcomes

**Findings without reported adverse effects in particular samples**

- LactMed reports no acute adverse effects in a group of **26 breastfed infants** whose mothers were being treated for severe depression. It also describes a cohort of **25 infants** with reported normal weight gain through six months and no abnormal effects reported by mothers. These were observational data, not randomized trials, and the samples were small. [S21](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

- The 1997 pharmacokinetic study of **11 mother–infant pairs** reported no observed adverse effects, but its main purpose was measuring exposure rather than detecting clinical outcomes. [S19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9286185/)

- The 2023 study of **60 mothers** reported no significant overall differences in birth outcomes or development through 12 months between exposed and nonexposed children. **Study type:** naturalistic observational study. **Source population:** mixed pregnancy, lactation, neonate, and child populations. The abstract does not isolate the results for sertraline exposure through breast milk, so this finding should not be generalized to breastfeeding-only sertraline exposure. [S14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36103361/)

**Reported adverse findings**

- **Case report:** LactMed describes a **preterm infant born at 33 weeks** whose mother used sertraline during gestation and postpartum. The infant developed hyperthermia, abnormal muscle-tone regulation, and high-pitched crying. Infant serum sertraline and metabolite concentrations were measured, and later testing identified genetically intermediate metabolism of two relevant enzymes. The report’s authors attributed the reaction probably to sertraline, but this is one case involving prematurity, prenatal exposure, breastfeeding exposure, and unusual metabolism; it cannot establish frequency or risk for term infants. [S21](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

- **Retrospective observational study:** Among **30 nursing mothers** prescribed sertraline and followed for at least eight weeks, adverse effects were reported in **5 infants (13%)**. Reported effects included insomnia, restlessness, constant crying, and poor feeding. The study relied on maternal reports, was small and uncontrolled, and cannot establish that sertraline caused every reported event. [S21](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

- LactMed also reports isolated cases of benign sleep myoclonus and transient agitation. One report involved infant sedation when the mother was also taking other potentially sedating medicines, so attribution to sertraline alone is uncertain. [S21](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

- A small study of **30 infants exposed to an SSRI during pregnancy**, including **4 exposed to sertraline**, reported blunted pain responses compared with infants of nondepressed, unexposed mothers. Because there was no comparison group of depressed mothers who were not taking medication, the result cannot distinguish medication effects from effects related to maternal depression or other factors. The study involved mixed prenatal and breastfeeding exposure, not breastfeeding-only exposure. [S21](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

### Important limitations and context

- Milk concentration, infant serum concentration, and estimated infant intake are **exposure measurements**; they are not themselves evidence that an infant experienced harm or benefit.
- “No adverse effects observed” means none were detected in the reported sample. It does not establish that uncommon effects cannot occur.
- The evidence base includes small observational studies, retrospective reports, case reports, and modeled or pooled analyses. Randomized trials designed to evaluate infant safety were not retrieved.
- The LactMed extract is incomplete: the infant-outcomes section remained truncated, and the “Effects on Lactation and Breastmilk” section was omitted by the tool’s character limit. Therefore, conclusions about effects on milk production or breastfeeding experience are incomplete in this lookup. [S21](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)
- The retrieved DailyMed label was for **SERTRALINE HYDROCHLORIDE TABLET [REMEDYREPACK INC.]**. Product matching was not confirmed, and several expected label sections were not returned. Its listed adverse reactions concern the medicine taker and should not automatically be interpreted as breastfed-infant effects. [S1](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=687ebdc4-6946-454e-8d46-3207488ccf32)

### Questions for a clinician or pharmacist

1. How well do these studies match the infant’s age, prematurity status, medical conditions, and degree of breastfeeding?
2. Which findings are measured exposure, and which are observed clinical outcomes?
3. Does the exact product and formulation match the retrieved label?
4. Could other medicines, supplements, prematurity, or underlying infant conditions contribute to any observed symptoms?
5. What follow-up information about feeding, growth, sleep, alertness, or development would be useful?
6. Which uncertainties in the evidence matter most for this particular parent–infant situation?
7. How should the parent’s treatment goals and the risks of changing treatment be incorporated into the discussion?

Stopping or changing a medicine can also carry risks and belongs in a conversation with the prescribing clinician.

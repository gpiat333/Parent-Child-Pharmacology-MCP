## Lamotrigine pregnancy evidence

**Bottom line:** Human pregnancy-registry data are generally reassuring for **overall major congenital malformations with first-trimester lamotrigine monotherapy**, but an isolated oral-cleft signal was reported in one registry and was not consistently replicated. The evidence is observational and predominantly from pregnancies involving **epilepsy**, so it does not establish an individualized risk estimate.

### Maternal effects and underlying disease

- No lamotrigine-specific maternal adverse-effect outcome estimate was retrieved in the selected label. The DailyMed label does, however, describe **epilepsy-related pregnancy risks**, including preeclampsia, preterm labor, bleeding, placental abruption, poor fetal growth, prematurity, fetal death, and maternal mortality. These are underlying-disease context, not evidence that lamotrigine caused those outcomes. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)
- A 2024 practice guideline covered **pregnancy and neonate populations** in people with epilepsy; it was a guideline based on systematic-review evidence, not a new primary lamotrigine trial. [S24](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38748979/)
- A 2025 narrative review covered **pregnancy in people with bipolar disorder**, but the retrieved abstract did not provide a lamotrigine-specific pregnancy outcome estimate. [S25](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40593436/)
- The label reports pregnancy-related changes in lamotrigine concentrations, but the retrieved brief did not provide quantitative human pharmacokinetic results. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

### Measured fetal or infant exposure

- No quantitative fetal concentration was retrieved. Registry enrollment documents exposure during pregnancy but does not measure fetal drug concentration.
- A naturalistic study of **60 mothers** with mixed psychotropic-medication exposure populations—pregnancy, lactation, neonates, and children—measured serum and breastmilk concentrations and followed children for 12 months. Its abstract reported a high breastmilk concentration-by-dose ratio for lamotrigine, but the retrieved text provided no lamotrigine-specific numeric milk concentration. This is lactation exposure evidence, not fetal exposure evidence. [S22](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36103361/)
- The label includes animal findings involving fetal folate concentrations; these are animal data and should not be generalized to human fetal exposure. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

### Observed fetal and infant outcomes

#### Major congenital malformations

The selected DailyMed label reports the following **first-trimester lamotrigine monotherapy** registry estimates:

- International Lamotrigine Pregnancy Registry: **2.2%** among **1,558 infants**; registry population: pregnancy, with most lamotrigine data from people with epilepsy.
- NAAED Pregnancy Registry: **2.0%** among **1,562 infants**; registry population: pregnancy, predominantly epilepsy.
- EURAP: **2.9%** among **2,514 exposures**; international pregnancy-registry population, with the exact source population not otherwise specified in the returned label field.

The label states that overall malformation frequency was similar to general-population estimates, while also noting that outcome definitions, ascertainment, and comparator groups limit conclusions. These are registry observations, not proof of no risk and not necessarily applicable to polytherapy or other underlying conditions. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

#### Oral clefts

- The NAAED registry reported isolated oral clefts in **3.2 per 1,000** among **2,200 infants** exposed early in pregnancy, described in the label as approximately threefold higher than an unexposed healthy-control comparison.
- The label states that this finding was **not observed in other large international pregnancy registries**.
- A European case-control study using 21 congenital-anomaly registries reported an adjusted odds ratio of **1.45** with a 95% confidence interval of **0.8–2.63**.

The oral-cleft evidence is therefore inconsistent across the returned sources rather than uniformly replicated. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

#### Other outcomes

The label states that meta-analyses evaluated fetal death, stillbirth, preterm birth, small-for-gestational-age birth, and neurodevelopmental delay. It reports no data suggesting increased risk with lamotrigine monotherapy, but explicitly limits interpretation because outcome definitions, ascertainment methods, and comparator groups differed. This is a label summary and limitation statement, not proof that these outcomes cannot occur. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

The mixed psychotropic-medication study reported no significant differences in birth outcomes or development during the first 12 months between exposed and nonexposed children, but its source population included pregnancy, lactation, neonates, and children and the abstract did not provide a lamotrigine-specific estimate for each outcome. [S22](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36103361/)

### Questions for the clinician

1. How closely do these registry populations match the underlying condition being treated—epilepsy versus bipolar disorder?
2. Does the evidence apply to the exact formulation and to monotherapy versus combination therapy?
3. How should the conflicting oral-cleft findings and their confidence intervals be interpreted?
4. Which comparator is most appropriate for this context: general population, disease-matched controls, or another antiseizure medication?
5. Which maternal, fetal, neonatal, and postpartum outcomes are most relevant to discuss?
6. What quantitative human pharmacokinetic data, if any, are available for the specific formulation and pregnancy stage?
7. Would contributing data to the NAAED Pregnancy Registry be relevant to this research discussion?

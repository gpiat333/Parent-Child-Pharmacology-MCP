## Pregnancy evidence brief: lamotrigine

**Identity:** The lookup matched lamotrigine exactly (RxCUI 28439). [S26](https://rxnav.nlm.nih.gov/REST/rxcui/28439/properties.json) Evidence below concerns lamotrigine as a single ingredient; combination-product safety and interactions were not assessed.

### Overall evidence

For **human pregnancy data**, the selected DailyMed label reports no increased overall frequency of major congenital malformations in several pregnancy registries among first-trimester lamotrigine-monotherapy exposures. Reported findings were:

- **2.2%** among 1,558 infants in the International Lamotrigine Pregnancy Registry
- **2.0%** among 1,562 infants in the NAAED Pregnancy Registry
- **2.9%** among 2,514 exposures in EURAP, an international registry

The label states that these frequencies were similar to general-population estimates. The underlying source population was pregnant women, with most lamotrigine pregnancy data coming from women with epilepsy. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

One NAAED analysis reported isolated oral clefts in **3.2 per 1,000** among 2,200 early-pregnancy exposures, described in the label as higher than an unexposed healthy-control group. The label also states that this finding was not observed in other large international registries; a European case-control analysis reported an adjusted odds ratio of 1.45 with a confidence interval including no association. These results are not fully consistent across studies. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

**Measured fetal or infant exposure**

- The label reports that pregnancy-related physiological changes can alter lamotrigine concentrations, including reports of lower concentrations during pregnancy and restoration toward pre-pregnancy concentrations after delivery. This is a source-label pharmacokinetic observation, not an individualized dosing recommendation. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)
- A naturalistic study of **60 mothers** measured psychotropic-drug concentrations in serum and breast milk and followed exposed children during the first 12 months. The returned source population included **pregnancy, lactation, neonates, and children**; the drug-specific lamotrigine subgroup size was **missing in the returned metadata**. The abstract reported a high breast-milk concentration-to-dose ratio for lamotrigine, but supplied no lamotrigine-specific numeric concentration in the retrieved text. [S22](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36103361/)
- No measured fetal concentration was retrieved.

**Observed fetal or infant outcomes**

- The DailyMed registry findings above concern pregnant women and their infants exposed to lamotrigine monotherapy, primarily in the first trimester. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)
- The label states that meta-analyses did not report an increased risk of major congenital malformations overall, but that differences in outcome definitions, ascertainment, and comparator groups limit conclusions about fetal death, stillbirth, preterm birth, small-for-gestational-age birth, and neurodevelopmental delay. [S10](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)
- The 60-mother naturalistic study—source population: pregnancy, lactation, neonates, and children—reported no significant differences in birth outcomes between in-utero-exposed and nonexposed newborns and no significant differences in development during the first 12 months. The retrieved abstract did not establish a lamotrigine-specific sample size, so this result should not be treated as a lamotrigine-only outcome estimate. [S22](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36103361/)

### Guidelines and reviews

A 2024 AAN/AES/SMFM practice guideline reviewed people with epilepsy of childbearing potential and outcomes in their children, including major congenital malformations, perinatal outcomes, and neurodevelopment. Its abstract states that lamotrigine is among the medicines clinicians may consider when appropriate, based on epilepsy syndrome, likelihood of seizure control, and comorbidities. This is a guideline statement, not an individualized treatment decision. [S24](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38748979/)

A 2023 epilepsy review described its source population as pregnancy and lactation and its study type as a review; its abstract characterized lamotrigine as relatively safe compared with several other antiseizure medicines, while noting that pregnancy safety evidence relies largely on registries and observational studies rather than large randomized trials. [S23](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37684052/)

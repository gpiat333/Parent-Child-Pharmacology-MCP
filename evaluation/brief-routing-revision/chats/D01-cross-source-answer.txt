## Sertraline during breastfeeding

**Identity:** The medication was matched exactly to sertraline; the retrieved identity record lists sertraline as the ingredient. [S9](https://rxnav.nlm.nih.gov/REST/rxcui/36437/properties.json)

### Measured milk and infant exposure

**LactMed — source population: lactating people and breastfed infants; study types mentioned include observational studies, case reports, and a meta-analysis.** The retrieved metadata does not establish a study count; reference counts are not study counts. [S10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

- Sertraline was generally measured at low concentrations in breast milk. Norsertraline, its metabolite, was also often detected.
- Across summarized studies, estimated relative infant exposure was generally low, commonly reported at approximately **0.2%–2.4% of the maternal weight-adjusted amount**. These are measured or calculated exposure estimates, not clinical outcomes.
- In several infant-serum studies, sertraline was undetectable; norsertraline was sometimes detectable at low levels.
- One reported **33-week preterm infant** had measurable sertraline and norsertraline in serum and symptoms described as similar to neonatal-abstinence symptoms. The source also reported genetically intermediate metabolism. This is a case report and does not establish causation for other infants.
- The returned LactMed extract is incomplete: the infant-effects section is truncated, and the section on effects on lactation and breastmilk was omitted by the retrieval limit. Therefore, this is not a complete LactMed record. [S10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

### Observed infant outcomes

- Several small observational groups reported no acute adverse effects during the study periods.
- One report described benign sleep myoclonus and spontaneously resolving agitation in an infant.
- One summarized follow-up reported normal six-month weight gain and no abnormal effects reported by the mothers.
- The LactMed summary states that no developmental adverse effects were found among infants followed for up to five years; the returned extract does not establish the complete study population or study count. [S10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)
- Findings involving exposure during pregnancy—such as possible neonatal withdrawal—should not be treated as outcomes caused by breast-milk exposure alone. The retrieved record includes mixed prenatal and lactation contexts. [S10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

### Questions to ask a clinician

- Does the selected label match my exact product and formulation?
- Which evidence applies specifically to breastfeeding rather than prenatal exposure or general adult use?
- How closely do the study populations match my infant’s age, gestational age, prematurity, health conditions, and feeding pattern?
- Which findings are measured exposure, and which are observed clinical outcomes?
- What uncertainties and missing evidence matter for my situation?
- What follow-up should be considered for both the lactating person and the infant?
- How should the benefits of treatment and the risks of changing treatment be weighed?

The retrieved MCP guidance notes that stopping or changing medication can also carry risks and belongs in a discussion with the prescribing clinician.

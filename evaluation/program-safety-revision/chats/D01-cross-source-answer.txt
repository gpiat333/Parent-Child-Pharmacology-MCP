## Sertraline during breastfeeding

The retrieved record confirms the ingredient as **sertraline**. LactMed is lactation-specific and does not provide pregnancy evidence. [S9](https://rxnav.nlm.nih.gov/REST/rxcui/36437/properties.json)

### Measured milk and infant exposure

- LactMed reports generally low sertraline concentrations in breast milk. The metabolite **norsertraline** was often detectable at low levels in infant serum. [S10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)
- In reported studies, infant serum sertraline was frequently undetectable, although some infants had measurable sertraline and/or norsertraline. Exposure estimates varied with sampling timing, breastfeeding extent, and study population. [S10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)
- One report involved a **preterm infant born at 33 weeks** with measured sertraline and norsertraline in serum. The record also describes genetically intermediate metabolism; the retrieved evidence does not establish that sertraline caused the reported symptoms. [S10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

These are measured exposure findings, not clinical outcomes by themselves.

### Observed infant outcomes

- Several small observational groups reported no observed acute adverse effects, and one group reported normal infant weight gain through 6 months. The retrieved LactMed metadata does not establish a study count; missing study counts are unknown, not zero. [S10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)
- LactMed reports one 4-month-old infant with benign sleep myoclonus and spontaneously resolving agitation. The source does not establish causation. [S10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)
- In the preterm-infant report, symptoms described as similar to neonatal abstinence occurred in the context of prematurity and genetically intermediate metabolism. The returned extract is insufficient to determine the causal contribution of sertraline. [S10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)
- LactMed’s summary reports no developmental adverse effects in infants followed for up to 5 years in the cited material, but this does not establish safety for every infant or circumstance. [S10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

**Completeness limitation:** The retrieved LactMed “Effects in Breastfed Infants” section is truncated, and “Effects on Lactation and Breastmilk” was omitted by the retrieval limit. This is not a complete LactMed record. [S10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

## Questions to ask a clinician

- Does the selected label match the exact product and formulation?
- How closely does the evidence match the infant’s age, prematurity status, health conditions, and extent of breastfeeding?
- Which findings are measured exposure, and which are observed clinical outcomes?
- What uncertainty remains because the LactMed extract is truncated?
- What parent and infant follow-up is appropriate in this situation?
- How should the benefits of treating the parent’s underlying condition and the risks of medication changes be considered together?

Stopping or changing medication can also carry risks and belongs in a discussion with the prescribing clinician.

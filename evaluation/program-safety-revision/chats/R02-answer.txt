Yes—these are different kinds of evidence.

**Measured exposure in milk**
- In five breastfeeding women, the peak milk concentration averaged **87.2 mcg/L**; the calculated weight-adjusted infant exposure was **3.5%**.
- In seven women who were at least one month postpartum, peak milk levels averaged **59.3 mcg/L**; the calculated weight-adjusted infant exposure averaged **0.7%**.
- Direct infant drug levels were **not reported**: LactMed states that relevant published information on infant levels was not found. These results describe measured milk concentrations and estimated milk intake—not observed infant effects. [S2](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501255/)

**Actually observed in infants**
- A report based on manufacturer correspondence stated that among **three women** who used sumatriptan while breastfeeding, **none reported adverse effects in their infants**. The dose and route were unspecified. [S2](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501255/)
- This is limited clinical-outcome information, not systematic infant examination or infant blood-level measurement. The LactMed record gives no established study count; that missing count should not be interpreted as zero studies or as proof of safety. [S2](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501255/)

Thus, milk measurements provide **pharmacokinetic exposure evidence**—how much drug was detected or estimated to enter milk—whereas the three reports provide limited **observed infant-outcome evidence**. The source population is lactating women; applicability to a particular infant, including a preterm infant, is not established by this record.

LactMed also separately reports maternal lactation-related events, including breast or nipple pain and occasional decreased milk production; these are not infant outcomes. Stopping or changing a medication can also carry risks and belongs in a prescribing-clinician conversation.

## Sertraline during lactation: balanced research summary

### Measured exposure: milk and infant serum

LactMed reports generally low sertraline concentrations in breast milk. Across the summarized pharmacokinetic studies, estimated relative infant exposure was usually low, but results varied with sampling time, breastfeeding extent, milk fraction, and study methods. Norsertraline, an active metabolite, was often detectable at low levels in infant serum. Milk concentrations could differ between foremilk and hindmilk.

Infant serum exposure was often undetectable but not always:

- In a study of 30 breastfed infants aged 6–13 weeks, 22 had undetectable sertraline serum levels and 8 had detectable levels.
- In a study of 22 breastfed infants, sertraline was detectable in 4 and norsertraline in 11.
- A pooled analysis included 53 mother–infant pairs, with wide variability in infant-to-maternal plasma concentrations.
- One case involved a preterm infant with measurable sertraline and norsertraline and symptoms described as resembling neonatal abstinence. The report also identified genetically intermediate metabolism.

These are **measured exposure findings**, not clinical outcomes. A detectable milk or serum concentration does not by itself establish harm. [S10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

### Observed infant outcomes and adverse findings

Reported infant findings included:

- One case of benign sleep myoclonus and agitation that resolved spontaneously.
- One preterm-infant case involving symptoms resembling neonatal abstinence, measurable drug/metabolite levels, and genetically intermediate metabolism.
- One report of sedation in an infant whose mother was also taking other medicines with sedating potential; the report does not establish that sertraline caused the sedation.
- A small study reported blunted pain responses among infants exposed to an SSRI during pregnancy and breastfeeding; only four infants were exposed to sertraline. The comparison group consisted of infants of nondepressed mothers, so effects related to maternal depression or other differences could not be excluded.
- An uncontrolled online survey of 930 breastfeeding mothers taking antidepressants reported perceived discontinuation-type symptoms in about 10% of infants. This was not sertraline-specific and relied on maternal reports, so it cannot establish a sertraline-specific frequency or causation.
- Other small groups reported no observed acute adverse effects, no clinically noted adverse reactions, or normal reported infant weight gain. LactMed states that no developmental adverse effects were found in infants followed up to 5 years, but the retrieved record does not establish a complete study count.

The adverse reports and the reports of no observed effects should be interpreted together. The evidence does not establish that every reported symptom was caused by sertraline, and absence of an observed effect in a small study does not prove safety for all breastfed infants. [S10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

### Relevant source context

The LactMed material concerns **lactation**. It notes that difficulty breastfeeding among people taking SSRIs during pregnancy and postpartum may partly reflect the underlying illness rather than a direct medication effect. It also distinguishes third-trimester prenatal SSRI exposure from exposure through breast milk; neonatal withdrawal findings associated with prenatal exposure should not automatically be attributed to lactation exposure. [S10](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501191/)

The retrieved DailyMed record describes adverse reactions in the **person taking sertraline**, not automatically in a breastfed infant. The selected record was for **SERTRALINE HYDROCHLORIDE TABLET [REMEDYREPACK INC.]**, with an effective date of November 5, 2026. The product match was not confirmed as the exact product used by the person in question. The retrieved adverse-reactions section included warnings such as bleeding risk, serotonin syndrome, seizures, hyponatremia, and discontinuation syndrome; several other expected label sections were not returned. [S1](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=687ebdc4-6946-454e-8d46-3207488ccf32)

The retrieved LactMed extract was incomplete: the infant-effects section was truncated, and the section on effects on lactation and breast milk was omitted by the retrieval limit. The study count was not established in the retrieved data. The evidence described includes case reports, observational research, and a meta-analysis, but the available record does not provide a complete, adjudicated study-by-study inventory. ### Questions for a clinician or pharmacist

1. Does the available evidence apply to the exact sertraline product and formulation?
2. How do the infant’s age, prematurity status, feeding pattern, and health conditions affect interpretation?
3. Which findings represent measured exposure, and which represent observed clinical outcomes?
4. Are any co-medications or maternal health conditions relevant to interpreting an infant finding?
5. Which uncertainties arise from small sample sizes, incomplete follow-up, or lack of a suitable comparison group?
6. What are the relevant benefits of effective treatment and the risks associated with changing treatment in this individual context?
7. What follow-up information would help distinguish a medication-related finding from normal infant variation or another cause?
8. Which portions of the evidence are missing because the LactMed extract was truncated?

### Maternal effects and underlying disease

The selected product label states that most human pregnancy data involve people with **epilepsy**. It lists epilepsy-associated risks—including preeclampsia, preterm labor, hemorrhage, placental abruption, poor fetal growth, prematurity, fetal death, and maternal mortality—as risks that may occur with epilepsy itself, particularly when convulsive seizures are untreated or poorly controlled. These findings should not be attributed to lamotrigine exposure alone. [S2](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

The label also reports that pregnancy-related physiological changes may alter lamotrigine concentrations and therapeutic effect. This is label context for clinician discussion, not an individualized dosing recommendation. No pregnancy-specific maternal adverse-reaction findings were supplied in the structured label fields; that field is **missing in the exported data**, not evidence that no such findings exist. [S2](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

The 2024 AAN/AES/SMFM practice guideline concerns people with epilepsy of childbearing potential and integrates fetal outcomes with seizure control. Its population is pregnancy/neonate, with the source age band not established in the returned metadata. It is a practice guideline, not a direct lamotrigine exposure cohort. [S15](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38748979/)

### Measured exposure

The retrieved human studies describe **maternal lamotrigine or antiseizure-medication exposure** and subsequent fetal or child outcomes. They do not report directly measured fetal lamotrigine concentrations in the returned abstracts. Therefore, measured fetal exposure cannot be summarized from these records.

The selected label reports animal developmental findings separately from human exposure data; animal exposure should not be treated as measured human fetal exposure. [S2](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

### Fetal and infant/child outcomes

#### Major congenital malformations

The selected DailyMed label summarizes registry estimates for first-trimester lamotrigine monotherapy:

- International Lamotrigine Pregnancy Registry: 2.2% among 1,558 exposed infants.
- NAAED Pregnancy Registry: 2.0% among 1,562 exposed infants.
- EURAP: 2.9% among 2,514 exposures.

The label states that overall malformation frequencies were similar to general-population estimates. It also reports an NAAED finding for isolated oral clefts—3.2 per 1,000 exposures, described in the label as approximately threefold higher than unexposed healthy controls—but notes that this finding was not observed in other large international registries. [S2](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

A prospective observational cohort of **pregnant people with epilepsy**—source population: pregnancy and adolescent/adult age labels—reported major congenital malformations in 110 of 3,584 lamotrigine-monotherapy pregnancies, or 3.1% (95% CI, 2.5%–3.7%). Offspring were assessed through 1 year after birth. This was an observational comparison across antiseizure medicines, not proof that lamotrigine caused or prevented any outcome. [S14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38497990/)

A 2025 study—an observational pregnancy study in people with epilepsy—compared 490 lamotrigine-monotherapy pregnancies with 214 pregnancies without antiseizure-medication exposure during at least the first half of pregnancy. Fetal malformations occurred in 4.49% versus 3.27%; the risk ratio was 1.37 (95% CI, 0.60–3.16). The result was not statistically significant, and the authors stated that a small effect could not be excluded. [S17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39862525/)

#### Later child neurodevelopment

A UK/Swedish registry cohort included pregnancy, infant, child, and adolescent source-population labels; the returned metadata did not establish a narrower age band. Its abstract reported little evidence that children exposed to lamotrigine in pregnancy were more likely to receive neurodevelopmental diagnoses. The abstract did not provide a lamotrigine-specific effect estimate in the retrieved record. [S16](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39548057/)

### Animal findings

The label reports animal findings including reduced fetal body weight, skeletal variations, neurobehavioral abnormalities, increased offspring mortality in one rat study, and reduced fetal folate concentrations. These are animal observations and cannot be treated as human fetal outcomes. [S2](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

### Limitations

- Most human evidence concerns epilepsy, so underlying disease, seizure control, co-medications, and other clinical factors may affect comparisons.
- Registry and cohort studies are observational; associations do not establish causation.
- Studies differ in exposure timing, monotherapy definitions, comparator groups, outcome definitions, and follow-up.
- The oral-cleft signal is inconsistent across data sources.
- The DailyMed pregnancy section was marked **truncated**, although the structured pregnancy fields supplied the principal registry findings.
- The selected label was one product label—“LAMOTRIGINE KIT … [OWP Pharmaceuticals, Inc.]”—with effective date October 12, 2026; it was not established as the most recent label or as representing every formulation. DailyMed’s returned limitation states that in-use labeling may differ from the latest FDA-approved labeling. [S2](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)
- The prototype lookup did not identify a curated federal pregnancy counterpart to LactMed; this is a limitation of the lookup, not proof that no other pregnancy resource exists. [S20](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/labeling/questions-and-answers-pregnancy-and-lactation-labeling-rule) [S21](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK501922/)

### Questions for the clinician

1. How should the risks associated with the underlying condition—especially epilepsy and seizure control—be weighed alongside the pregnancy-outcome evidence?
2. Does the available evidence apply to the specific indication, formulation, gestational timing, and monotherapy or polytherapy context?
3. How should the inconsistent oral-cleft findings be interpreted?
4. Which fetal and child outcomes are most relevant to the discussion: malformations, fetal growth, preterm birth, neonatal outcomes, or longer-term neurodevelopment?
5. What pregnancy and postpartum concentration or clinical-response information is relevant for this individual situation?

## Lamotrigine pregnancy evidence

**Evidence status:** Human pregnancy evidence was retrieved, but it is not a safety verdict and cannot determine an individual treatment plan.

### Maternal effects and underlying disease

The selected DailyMed product label is **LAMOTRIGINE KIT (LAMOTRIGINE) KIT LAMOTRIGINE TABLET**, OWP Pharmaceuticals, Inc.; effective date **October 12, 2026**. It is one selected product label, not all lamotrigine formulations. The label states that most pregnancy data come from people with **epilepsy** and separately describes epilepsy-associated risks—including preeclampsia, preterm labor, hemorrhage, placental abruption, poor fetal growth, prematurity, fetal death, and maternal mortality. These are underlying-disease and disease-control considerations in the label, not outcomes shown to be caused by lamotrigine. [S12](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

In the MONEAD prospective observational cohort of pregnant women with epilepsy, maternal lamotrigine exposure measures changed during pregnancy. The analyzed population included **326 pregnant participants** and **104 nonpregnant controls**; dose-normalized concentrations decreased by up to **56.1%** for lamotrigine compared with postpartum values. [S33](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35157004/)

A related MONEAD prospective observational pharmacokinetic cohort included **170 pregnant** and **51 nonpregnant women with epilepsy**. The study reported substantial modeled clearance changes in approximately 91% of pregnant participants, while approximately 9% had little or no significant modeled change. This is pharmacokinetic evidence, not an individualized dosing recommendation. [S41](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39764656/)

### Measured fetal or infant exposure

No direct numeric **human fetal lamotrigine concentrations** were retrieved in the pregnancy studies reviewed. The retrieved pharmacokinetic studies measured maternal plasma concentrations and modeled maternal clearance. Therefore, measured maternal exposure should not be treated as a measured fetal exposure estimate. [S33](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35157004/) [S41](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39764656/)

### Observed fetal and child outcomes

**Major congenital malformations**

The selected label reports first-trimester lamotrigine-monotherapy registry estimates of:

- **2.2%** among 1,558 infants in the International Lamotrigine Pregnancy Registry;
- **2.0%** among 1,562 infants in the NAAED Pregnancy Registry; and
- **2.9%** among 2,514 exposures in EURAP.

The label reports that these registries did not show an overall increased malformation frequency or consistent malformation pattern. It also reports an isolated-oral-cleft signal in the NAAED registry—**3.2 per 1,000** among 2,200 early-pregnancy exposures—but notes that this was not observed in other large international registries. [S12](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=474fb321-575c-4b97-9dbe-080061652ee4)

A 2024 prospective observational longitudinal cohort of people with epilepsy, including adolescents and adults, followed offspring through one year after birth. Among **3,584 lamotrigine-monotherapy pregnancies**, major congenital malformations occurred in **110 (3.1%; 95% CI 2.5%–3.7%)**. [S38](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38497990/)

A 2025 observational study of **490 lamotrigine-monotherapy pregnancies** and **214 pregnancies in women with epilepsy without antiseizure-medication exposure during at least the first half of pregnancy** reported fetal malformations in **4.49% versus 3.27%**, respectively; the risk ratio was **1.37 (95% CI 0.60–3.16)**. The reported difference was not statistically significant, but the authors stated that a small association could not be excluded. [S42](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39862525/)

**Pregnancy loss and stillbirth**

The EURAP prospective observational registry included pregnancies exposed to multiple antiseizure-medication monotherapies and polytherapy; the returned abstract reports **7,055 monotherapy pregnancies** and **1,415 polytherapy pregnancies**. Intrauterine death rates were compared across treatment groups, but a lamotrigine-specific intrauterine-death rate was not provided in the returned abstract. The reported overall monotherapy rate was **8.2%**, compared with **12.1%** for polytherapy. These findings should not be assigned specifically to lamotrigine. [S28](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26187231/)

**Neurodevelopment**

The NaME prospective observational cohort recruited **401 pregnant women with epilepsy** in the United Kingdom; **277 children** completed the 24-month Bayley assessment. After adjustment, prenatal lamotrigine-monotherapy exposure was not associated with poorer 24-month cognitive, language, or motor scores compared with nonexposed children. The study population was women with epilepsy and their infants, with follow-up to 24 months; the abstract states that later-emerging effects require further follow-up. [S35](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37403560/)

The MONEAD prospective observational cohort included **302 children of women with epilepsy** and **84 children of women without epilepsy** in the primary analysis. Overall adaptive functioning at 4.5 years did not differ significantly between the two groups. Within the epilepsy-exposed group, higher third-trimester antiseizure-medication concentrations were associated with lower adaptive-functioning scores; a lamotrigine-specific association was reported, but this was observational and does not establish causation. [S37](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37983058/)

A large UK/Swedish registry cohort included **3,182,773 children**, including **17,495 exposed to antiseizure medications during pregnancy**. The abstract reported little evidence that children exposed to lamotrigine were more likely to receive neurodevelopmental diagnoses. This was a mixed population of infants, children, adolescents, and adults’ offspring—not a lamotrigine-only cohort. [S40](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39548057/)

### Questions for the clinician

1. How should the underlying epilepsy or bipolar-disorder risks be weighed alongside the pregnancy-outcome evidence?
2. Which studies best match the relevant indication, gestational timing, monotherapy/polytherapy status, and co-medications?
3. How should the observed pregnancy-related pharmacokinetic variability be interpreted for this person’s clinical follow-up?
4. How should the conflicting oral-cleft findings and differing comparator groups be explained?
5. Which pregnancy exposure registry or other outcome documentation is appropriate?
6. How should postpartum pharmacokinetic changes and continuity of disease control be addressed?
